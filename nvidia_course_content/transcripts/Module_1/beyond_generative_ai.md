# Beyond Generative AI

In this unit, we'll move beyond generative AI and explore the next frontiers, ​agentic AI, physical AI, ​and the AI factories that enable their deployment at scale. ​These are the technologies shaping the future of enterprise automation and robotics. ​Here's what we'll cover in this unit. ​We'll start by defining agentic AI and see how it differs from other forms of AI. ​Next, we'll look at physical AI and how intelligence is embodied in machines. ​Finally, we will explore AI factories, ​the scalable infrastructure making this all possible. 
​By the end of this unit, ​you'll be able to explain the key concepts of agentic AI, ​physical AI, and AI factories, ​describe how they work together, ​and recognize their impact on enterprise and industrial automation. ​We have a lot to cover, so let's get started. ​In 2012, a significant turning point in AI arrived when ​AlexNet won the ImageNet Large-Scale Visual Recognition Challenge. ​AlexNet was one of the first deep convolutional neural networks to leverage GPUs for training, ​dramatically reducing training time and making it feasible to train on large datasets like ImageNet. ​This marked the beginning of what is known as Perception AI. ​In 2022, OpenAI launched ChatGPT, ​five years after the introduction of the Transformer architecture in 2017. ​Models like ChatGPT were able to create new content from human input ​and brought the concept of generative AI into the mainstream. 
​The evolution from generative AI to agentic AI marks a pivotal transition towards autonomous systems. ​This shift moves beyond simple content generation to AI that can independently reason, ​plan, and execute a series of actions to achieve complex tasks. ​Physical AI marks the next evolutionary step. ​Physical AI refers to the artificial intelligence systems that are embodied in physical agents, ​allowing them to interact with and adapt to the real world. ​Let's dive deeper into agentic and physical AI. ​Let's start by talking about agentic AI. ​Artificial intelligence is leading us into an exciting new technological era that has only just begun. 
​A decade ago, perceptive AI gave us models that could find patterns and anomalies in data and make predictions. ​Is this a picture of a dog or a cat? Is there a bad cell in the scan? ​With generative AI, our models can create new, never-before-seen content, ​shaped by our input, so each one of us can be a creator. ​But the new promise of AI is to equip each one of us with digital agents who learn from us ​and can perceive, reason things out in multiple steps, ​and then make autonomous decisions on our behalf, without you having to do anything. ​Think of it like a super smart helper that can talk to other AI helpers to get things done. ​For example, in a store, one AI could figure out what customers want to buy, ​another could check what's in stock, and a third could make sure the shelves are always full. 
​All of this happens automatically, without people needing to do the work. ​But now, picture every office worker having an AI team like this. ​These AI assistants would be the experts in different areas, ​helping people tackle big, complicated tasks with much less effort. ​This is where AI is heading next, ​and it's going to change how businesses and tech companies operate in a big way. ​Now, let's see how AI agents differ from the AI we see today. ​First, AI agents are specialized and built for a specific task. ​They are trained, customized, they have guardrails, ​and they'll have access to domain-specific knowledge that will help them do their job well. 
​Different agents will have different levels of autonomy. ​Agents receive a prompt from either a user or a machine, or another agent, ​and they will break down that complex request into a plan, essentially creating a task list. ​As they work through their task list, agents have access to tools. ​They may do a web search, they can grab data from a vector database, ​they can do an SQL query, or perform a calculation. ​Guardrails can be built into AI agents to help ensure they execute tasks correctly. ​For example, a customer service AI agent may be able to process claims up to a certain amount, ​while claims above the amount would have to be approved by a human. ​As the agent goes through these processes, it has memory. 
​That is, the agent remembers what it does so it can operate as efficiently as possible. ​Agentic AI continuously improves through a feedback loop, or data flywheel, ​where the data generated from its interactions is fed into the system to enhance models. ​The agent critiques its own work. ​For example, is that answer good enough? ​If not, it'll go back, replan, and keep going through this iterative process ​until it produces a quality response back to the user or to the other agent. ​As you can see, this feedback loop enables agents to get better over time. ​But agentic AI isn't just about individual agents. 
​Let's explore how teams of specialized agents ​can work together to automate even more complex workflows. ​An agentic AI system can be complex. ​Often, a user will interact with a higher-level orchestration agent. ​They will interact with multiple downstream agents ​that have access to different databases and tools. ​For example, you can have a bug-tracking agent in a software development lifecycle, ​which may receive a customer issue from a bug-tracking database. ​This agent can work with other agents like a coding agent to fix the software ​and a technical docs agent to document the changes and software enhancements. ​Because this network of agents that can access different data sources and tools ​can become a complex process, ​the orchestration of agentic AI is going to be an area of significant importance. 
​Furthermore, when agents collaborate with one another, ​the ability to process tasks at maximum speed becomes crucial. ​Accelerated computing and the development of advanced AI factories ​will therefore be essential to enable these networks of agents ​to operate efficiently and cohesively. ​Orchestrating these agent teams efficiently is critical, ​especially as tasks and data sources grow more complex. ​Next, let's examine why advanced reasoning ​is a key ingredient for building truly capable AI agents. ​Reasoning models extend beyond simple prompt responses ​to deliver comprehensive capabilities including contextual understanding, ​multi-source data integration, answer validation, ​ambiguity resolution, and dynamic adaptation. ​Let's go over each of these in more detail. ​Contextual Understanding ​AI agents must comprehend the context underlying queries and data, ​including user intent, conversation history, and relevant background information. 
​Strong reasoning enables agents to maintain contextual awareness ​across multiple interactions and sequential steps. ​Multi-source Data Integration ​Advanced reasoning allows AI agents to synthesize information from diverse data sources, ​including text documents, databases, and real-time streams, ​creating more informed and accurate responses. ​Answer Validation ​Reasoning mechanisms verify that generated answers are both logically coherent ​and factually sound, which is essential for establishing trust in AI outputs. ​Ambiguity Resolution ​Real-world scenarios often present incomplete and conflicting information ​without clear-cut solutions. ​Reasoning capabilities enable AI to pose clarifying questions ​or make probabilistic assessments when direct answers aren't readily available. ​Dynamic Adaptation ​When conditions or user requirements evolve, ​reasoning-enabled agents can modify their approach rather than adhering to rigid scripts. ​They revise strategies in response to new information or changing objectives. 
​With this foundation in reasoning and adaptability, ​agentic AI is poised to tackle real-world challenges. ​Now, let's shift our focus from digital environments to the physical world ​and see how these concepts extend into physical AI. ​With a solid understanding of agentic AI, ​let's now explore how intelligence is embodied in the physical world through physical AI. ​Across every major industry, we're seeing a shift towards software-defined operations ​with companies building robotic systems at every scale. ​Digital twins and physical AI are being applied from individual assets and factory lines ​to entire fleets, warehouses, networks, and even city infrastructure. ​Industries like transportation, manufacturing, retail, ​supply chain, and telecommunications are all leveraging these technologies. ​By combining these, organizations can design, ​simulate, and optimize robotic systems, no matter the scale. 
​This approach helps them adapt quickly, ​improve efficiency, and stay competitive in a rapidly changing world. ​Let's look at the foundational industry shifts that are driving this demand for physical AI. ​The rapid adoption of physical AI is being fueled by three major industry trends. ​First, there's a persistent labor shortage. ​Analysts estimate a 50 million worker gap globally, which is pushing companies to ​automate more tasks and rely on robots for essential operations. ​Second, onshoring is accelerating, with new factories being built closer to home. ​This makes speed to deployment critical, and digital twins allow organizations to ​design and optimize these facilities virtually before investing in physical infrastructure. 
​We will explore the concept of digital twins later in this unit. ​Third, robotics innovation is moving away from pre-programmed, single-use machines ​to generalist, autonomous agents and robots that can reason and adapt to new tasks. ​Now, let's explore what makes physical AI models unique compared to large ​language models and how they generate actions in the real world. ​Unlike large language models that produce text, physical AI models generate the next ​best action based on real-time sensory input and intent. ​This enables autonomous machines like robots, self-driving vehicles, and smart infrastructure ​to perceive, understand, and interact with complex physical environments. ​Today, physical AI powers three main types of robots. ​Infrastructure robots for example. 
​Infrastructure robots for factories and warehouses. ​General purpose robots, including humanoids. ​And transportation robots, such as autonomous cars and robo-taxis. ​These systems are trained using high-fidelity simulations and digital twins, allowing them ​to safely learn and practice new skills at superhuman speed before being deployed in ​the real world. ​To support these capabilities, industries need powerful computing solutions tailored ​for each domain. ​Let's look at how the three-computer solution addresses this need. ​To make physical AI possible at scale, industries rely on three specialized types of computers, ​each playing a critical role. 
​First, supercomputers are used to train and fine-tune AI models, leveraging massive data ​sets to teach robots how to perceive and act in complex environments. ​Second, simulation computers generate synthetic data and allow developers to test and validate ​robotic behavior in virtual worlds before deployment, dramatically reducing risk and ​development time. ​Third, robot runtime computers are embedded in robots and vehicles, providing the real-time ​intelligence needed for perception, planning, and control as these machines operate in the ​physical world. ​This three-computer architecture—training, simulation, and runtime—forms the backbone ​for deploying robust, adaptable physical AI across facilities, general-purpose robots, ​and transportation systems. ​Next, we'll see how digital twins and simulation environments further accelerate development ​and deployment of these intelligent, physically-embodied AI systems. ​A digital twin is a dynamic, virtual representation of a physical system or environment that is ​continuously updated with real-world data. ​This living model reflects the current state of its physical counterpart, whether that's ​a single robot, a fleet of vehicles, a factory, or even a city infrastructure. 
​By mirroring real-world operations, digital twins enable organizations to monitor performance, ​predict maintenance needs, and optimize processes in a safe, virtual space before making changes ​in the real world. ​The power of a digital twin lies in its ability to provide real-time insights and support ​data-driven decision-making, helping enterprises reduce risk and improve efficiency. ​Now, let's explore how digital twins are being used across industries to transform ​the way we design, deploy, and manufacture AI. ​Digital twins are being adopted across a wide range of industries, from manufacturing and ​logistics to energy and transportation. ​By creating accurate, real-time, virtual replicas of assets and operations, organizations can ​simulate scenarios, test them, and then implement them. ​Digital twins are being used across a wide range of industries, from manufacturing and ​logistics to energy and transportation. ​improvements and monitor systems at scale. Now, let's see how combining physical AI and Digital Twins ​creates a powerful feedback loop, enabling smarter, safer, and more adaptive ​operations than either could achieve alone. 
​Physical AI and Digital Twins are most powerful when used together. Physical AI ​brings intelligence and autonomy to machines, allowing them to perceive, learn, ​and act in the real world. Digital Twins provide a real-time virtual environment ​where these systems can be monitored, simulated, and optimized safely. When ​integrated, Digital Twins give physical AI agents the context and boundaries ​they need to operate safely and efficiently, while physical AI agents ​make Digital Twins more actionable by continuously learning from real-world ​data and adapting to new scenarios. ​Having seen how physical AI and Digital Twins work together, let's explore why ​industries require an AI factory to scale and manage these intelligent ​systems. In order to understand why industries need AI factories, let's start ​by taking a look at the AI scaling laws. The rapid progress in AI is powered by a ​set of principles known as the AI scaling laws. These laws describe how ​model performance improves as we increase three things. The size of our ​models, the amount of training data, and the compute power available. ​The first law, pre-training scaling, shows that larger models trained on more data ​with more compute consistently yield better results. This drove the initial ​wave of investment in massive AI training infrastructure. The second law, ​post-training scaling, highlights the need for additional compute to fine-tune and ​adapt these models for specific real-world applications, further ​increasing infrastructure demands. The third and most recent law, test time ​scaling, sometimes called long thinking, is driven by reasoning models that use ​much more compute during inference. These models generate and evaluate ​multiple intermediate steps before producing a final answer, consuming up to ​100 times more compute than traditional inference. Together, these scaling laws ​are driving an exponential increase in the demand for specialized, efficient ​compute infrastructure, far beyond what traditional data centers were designed ​to handle. This is why industries are turning to purpose-built AI factories to ​keep up with the pace of innovation and deliver next-generation intelligent ​systems at scale. As organizations move from AI experimentation to large-scale ​deployment, they face a new set of challenges that traditional IT ​infrastructure wasn't designed to solve. First, the design process for AI systems ​is complex. Enterprises must prioritize use cases, ensure high-quality usable ​data, and select the right infrastructure, whether that's on-premises, in the cloud, ​or a hybrid approach. Second, deployment and operational costs become significant ​as AI models and data volumes grow. Building and maintaining the necessary ​infrastructure requires careful planning and resource allocation to avoid ​bottlenecks and excessive spending. Third, time to value is critical. Businesses ​want to move quickly from concept to production, but AI workloads require an ​optimized end-to-end stack, spanning networking, compute, and software, to ​deliver results efficiently and reliably. Unlike traditional workloads, AI places ​unique demands on every layer of the stack, making a purpose-built, integrated ​approach essential. That's where the concept of an AI factory has emerged, to ​streamline development, deployment, and scaling of AI solutions for real ​business impact. As AI factories become the new standard, let's look at how the ​exponential growth in inference compute requirements is driving their adoption. 
​The compute requirements for AI inference are growing at an unprecedented ​rate, driven by three key trends. First, models themselves are getting ​dramatically larger. Some have trillions of parameters, which means it takes much ​more compute power to generate responses. Second, the rise of long-thinking or ​inference time scaling is fundamentally changing how models operate. Reasoning ​models, like DeepSeq's R1, don't just generate a single answer. They create and ​evaluate multiple intermediate steps before selecting the best response. This ​process can require up to 100 times more compute than traditional inference. Third, ​models are now able to process much larger context, sometimes handling over ​100,000 input tokens at once. For example, Google's Gemini model can process the ​equivalent of 5,000 pages of text in a single prompt. This larger context window ​further increases the compute memory bandwidth needed for real-time AI ​applications. These trends are pushing the limits of traditional data centers, ​making it clear the purpose-built AI factories are needed to efficiently ​support the next generation of generative and agentic AI workloads. Next, ​we'll look at how this compounding demand for AI compute is shaping the ​future of infrastructure and the role of the AI factory. 
​The demand for AI compute isn't just rising, it's compounding, driven by the ​interplay of scaling laws and rapid advances in model architecture. As we ​scale up model size, data set size, and compute power, each dimension amplifies ​the others, resulting in exponential growth in the resources required for ​both training and inference. Over the past decade, compute use for state-of-the-art ​AI models has increased by a factor of about 5x each year, leading to a ​billion-fold increase overall. This growth is fueled by two main factors, the ​development of more powerful GPUs and specialized AI accelerators, and smarter ​algorithms that make more efficient use of available compute. Together, they ​enable more capable models, but also drive up infrastructure requirements at ​every level. As a result, traditional data centers are ​struggling to keep pace, and organizations are increasingly turning ​to purpose-built AI factories, integrated environments designed to handle the ​unique and escalating demands of modern AI workloads. Next, let's see how AI ​factories are enabling enterprises to manufacture intelligence at scale and ​keep up with this accelerating demand. 
​Just as traditional factories transformed raw materials into finished ​goods, AI factories are designed to manufacture intelligence at scale for ​the enterprise. The process begins with foundational models, secure customer data, ​and specialized AI tools. These are the raw materials. Within the AI factory, ​these elements are combined and refined. Models are fine-tuned and prototyped to ​address specific business needs, ensuring that the resulting AI solutions ​are tailored to the organization's data, vocabulary, and workflows. Once a ​customized model is ready, it can be deployed into production applications ​where it delivers real-time insights, automation, and decision support. ​Importantly, the AI factory doesn't stop there. New data from these production ​systems continually flows back into the factory, enabling ongoing fine-tuning and ​improvement. This creates a data flywheel where every cycle makes the ​model smarter and more valuable. Underpinning this entire process is a ​robust infrastructure, accelerated computing, high-speed networking, and an ​enterprise data platform that supports the full AI lifecycle from development to ​deployment. With this approach, organizations can rapidly iterate, deploy, ​and scale AI solutions, turning AI from a long-term investment into an immediate ​driver of competitive advantage in the next era of enterprise intelligence. 
​To deliver intelligence at scale, AI factories require an integrated, end-to-end ​stack that brings together hardware, software, networking, and ecosystem partners. ​Building a truly effective AI factory means going beyond just powerful servers ​or GPUs. It requires a comprehensive, full-stack approach. At the foundation is ​compute, with advanced GPUs and CPUs delivering the raw processing power ​needed for training, fine-tuning, and inference of massive AI models. ​Networking is equally critical. High-speed, low-latency connections using ​technologies like InfiniBand and advanced Ethernet ensure data flows ​seamlessly between compute nodes, storage, and users, supporting the immense ​throughput AI workloads demand. On top of this, software orchestrates every layer ​of the stack, from infrastructure management and workload scheduling to AI ​frameworks and development tools. This software layer enables automation, ​monitoring, and optimization across the entire AI lifecycle. When all these ​components are integrated and validated together, organizations can confidently ​scale their AI initiatives, reduce complexity, and accelerate time to value.
