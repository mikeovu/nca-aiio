Lesson 1 of 3Generative AI Overview

What you should be able to do after this unit

  1. Explain what generative AI is and how the technology works.
  2. Discuss generative AI market trends and the challenges in this space.
  3. Describe the steps enterprises take to unlock new business opportunities with generative AI.

1. What Generative AI Is

  • Generative AI is a subset of AI that creates new data or content, such as text, images and multimedia, from patterns learned in a dataset.
  • What sets it apart is versatility. It goes well beyond text and chat: it can generate realistic images, video, music, even whole virtual environments.

Use cases by industry

Industry Generative AI use cases
Finance Fraud detection, personalized banking, investment insights
Healthcare Molecule simulation, drug discovery, clinical trial data analysis
Retail Personalized shopping, automated catalog descriptions, automatic price optimization
Manufacturing Factory simulation, product design, predictive maintenance

2. Foundation Models

  • Foundation models are the base that generative AI systems are built from. They provide the starting framework for language structure, semantics and context.
  • They're neural networks trained on massive unlabeled datasets, and they handle many jobs: generating text and images, summarizing, translating and more.
  • Key breakthrough: they can use unsupervised or semi-supervised learning. The model finds patterns and structure on its own, so it doesn't need labeled data.
  • Unlabeled data is raw text, images, audio or video with no predefined categories, labels or annotations. It's abundant and diverse, coming from the internet, social media and proprietary datasets.

Example foundation models

Model What it does
DALL-E Realistic images from text descriptions. Also image captioning, editing and manipulation
Edify A diffusion model that makes photorealistic images from any text prompt
Llama 2 Diverse, high-quality natural-language text for content creation, language understanding and conversational AI
NVIDIA GPT A family of production-ready LLMs you can tune for enterprise apps: product descriptions, customer Q&A, writing code
GPT-4 Human-like text and content, images and music, plus conversational Q&A

3. How LLMs Work

  • LLMs are a turning point. They learn context, infer intent, can be independently creative, and can be fine-tuned for many different tasks.
  • They're built on a neural network architecture called the transformer.
  • Two stages: pre-training on huge text datasets, then fine-tuning for specific tasks.
  • Goal of a language model: predict the next word given the words that come before it. This works for human languages and for programming languages.

Generating text, step by step

  1. The user gives a prompt, e.g. "Write a review about an Italian restaurant I visited and enjoyed."
  2. The prompt is broken into tokens, which are fed into the model.
  3. The model predicts the next word from the tokens so far, then feeds it back in. It generates one word at a time.
  4. This repeats until the user stops or the model reaches a predetermined stopping point.

Key mechanics

  • Tokens are the smallest units of meaning: words, characters, sub-words or other symbols.
  • Self-attention is how the transformer understands relationships between tokens and concepts. It assigns each token a score, called a weight, to capture how strongly items relate.

Why GPUs

  • Generative AI is full of complex math and heavy computation.
  • GPUs excel at parallel processing, matrix operations, memory capacity and memory bandwidth.
  • The result is faster training and inference than CPUs, the ability to work with large-scale models, and support for real-time applications.
  • Example: ChatGPT was trained on 10,000 NVIDIA GPUs for weeks.

4. Challenges and Risks

Adoption involves technical, ethical and regulatory issues. Guardrails, meaning rules, mitigate inappropriate outcomes.

Risk What to watch for
Data privacy Watch healthcare and finance use cases closely to prevent leaks of money-related or sensitive data
IP rights and copyright Platforms should mitigate infringement of creators' work
Bias, errors and limitations Models are as biased as people, because they're trained on our own biases
Ethical implications It's unclear who is responsible for harmful output: the developers, the users or the technology
Malevolent activities Bad actors can use it too, e.g. fraudulent scams

5. Generative AI in the Enterprise

  • Enterprises that used traditional AI for classification, named entity recognition (NER) and NLP are gradually moving those tasks to LLMs.

Generative AI vs. traditional AI

Generative AI Traditional AI
Does Produces new content from learned patterns Detects patterns, makes decisions, analytics, classifies data, detects fraud
Compute Heavy Less compute-intensive
Relationship Complementary, not mutually exclusive Still the right fit for many use cases

The customization spectrum (from least to most investment)

Level What the enterprise does Investment
Generative AI as a service (the largest group, e.g. ChatGPT) Writes a prompt and gets a response. If the answer is poor, they refine the prompt. The model doesn't change Lowest
Moderate customization Adds parameters to an existing pre-trained model and slightly tunes it Needs infrastructure expertise and investment
Extensive customization Builds its own foundation model, with extensive fine-tuning Most infrastructure and AI expertise
  • Building a foundation model from scratch takes huge amounts of data and compute, so it's very costly. It's usually done by big tech, research institutions and well-funded startups.
  • Customizing a pre-trained model needs less data, fewer resources and less expertise. You feed it domain-specific data and tasks and adjust its parameters, which still requires knowing the model's capabilities, data format and evaluation metrics.
  • The most common choice: take an existing foundation model and fine-tune it, because it's more cost-effective.

Why building custom LLMs is hard

  1. Data: you need massive, diverse, high-quality data, and the collection, curation, storage and management that go with it.
  2. Compute: training at this scale needs large, expensive infrastructure.
  3. Software: you need tools for both training and inference, from algorithm development to accelerating inference on distributed infrastructure.
  4. Expertise: you need teams skilled in AI, ML and data science, who are hard to find and keep.

6. Getting Started: 5 Steps for Enterprises

  1. Identify the business opportunity. Pick use cases with big business impact that your unique data can improve.
  2. Build out domain and AI teams. Pair internal people with partner and application-provider AI expertise to form an interdisciplinary team.
  3. Analyze data for training and customization. Acquire, refine and protect the data.
  4. Invest in accelerated infrastructure. Assess current infrastructure, architecture and operating model, including cost and energy consumption.
  5. Develop a plan for responsible AI. Use tools and best practices so models act ethically.

In short, deploying generative AI isn't only about technology. It means aligning business goals, team capabilities, data strategy, infrastructure readiness and responsible AI.

7. Building a Generative AI App: the LLM Workflow

# Phase What happens
1 Data acquisition Collect data from public datasets, web scraping, user-generated content or proprietary data. It must be diverse and representative of the target domain
2 Data curation Clean, filter and organize the data
3 Pre-training Expose the model to a vast text corpus so it learns language patterns. This usually starts from a foundation model
4 Customization Adapt the generic model to a specific task or domain for better accuracy, efficiency and effectiveness
5 Model evaluation Measure how well the model learned and how accurately it predicts on unseen data
6 Inference Deploy the model to turn input into output such as classifications, predictions or recommendations
7 Guardrails Add these for ethical, safe, responsible use, reducing misuse and misinterpretation of generated text

8. The NVIDIA Generative AI Platform

It rests on three things: robust hardware, versatile software and enterprise-grade support. Together they make a production-ready way to build custom LLMs for language, multimodal, healthcare, life sciences and visual content.

Think of it as a pyramid, from top to bottom:

Layer What it is
NVIDIA AI Foundations (top) Tools and services for customizing enterprise generative AI:
• NeMo: text / language
• Picasso: visual content
• BioNeMo: biology
NVIDIA AI Enterprise (middle) A software suite that streamlines development and deployment of generative AI, computer vision and speech AI, so teams spend less time on maintenance and tuning
Accelerated compute infrastructure (base) Flexible. Runs anywhere: cloud or on-premises

Key Terms

  • Generative AI: AI that creates new content (text, images, video, music, virtual environments) from learned patterns.
  • Foundation model: a large neural network pre-trained on massive unlabeled data that serves as the base for many tasks.
  • Unsupervised / semi-supervised learning: learning patterns without labels, or with only a few.
  • LLM: a large language model, a transformer-based model that predicts the next token.
  • Transformer: the neural network architecture behind LLMs. It uses self-attention.
  • Self-attention: assigns weights between tokens to model how they relate.
  • Token: the smallest unit of meaning: a word, sub-word, character or symbol.
  • Prompt: the user's input that starts generation.
  • Pre-training vs. fine-tuning / customization: general learning on huge data vs. adapting the model to a specific domain or task.
  • Diffusion model: a type of image-generation model, e.g. Edify.
  • Guardrails: rules that keep model output safe, ethical and appropriate.
  • NeMo / Picasso / BioNeMo: NVIDIA AI Foundations services for language / visual / biology.

Exam Tips

  • Foundation models train on unlabeled data with unsupervised or semi-supervised learning. Contrast this with Unit 2's labeled-data training.
  • An LLM's core job is to predict the next word/token. Know that the transformer and self-attention are the mechanism behind it.
  • GPUs speed up both training and inference because of parallelism, matrix math, memory capacity and bandwidth.
  • Generative and traditional AI are complementary. Traditional AI is less compute-intensive and still fits many use cases.
  • Customization spectrum: as a service (prompt only, model unchanged), then moderate (tune a pre-trained model), then extensive (build your own foundation model). Fine-tuning an existing model is the cost-effective norm.
  • The 4 barriers to custom LLMs: data, compute, software and expertise.
  • The 5 enterprise steps: opportunity, teams, data, infrastructure, responsible AI.
  • The NVIDIA stack from top to bottom: AI Foundations (NeMo, Picasso, BioNeMo), then AI Enterprise, then accelerated infrastructure (cloud or on-prem).

Lesson 2 of 3Generating Images Using Generative AI

What this demo covers

A hands-on walkthrough of making and editing images from text prompts with a model hosted on ai.nvidia.com. It's light on theory, but it defines the image-generation parameters you should be able to recognize.

1. Where the models live

  • ai.nvidia.com → Try Now shows models grouped by application and industry (gaming, healthcare and so on). Image models are under Visual Design.
  • Models differ in what they do:
    • most turn text into 2D images
    • some turn text into 3D models
    • some turn a single image into a short video
  • Each model has its own set of parameters.
  • The demo model is Getty Images, which makes high-resolution 4K images. It was trained on NVIDIA's Edify platform using Getty Images' library (Edify is the diffusion model from Unit 3).

2. Model features

Feature What it does
Text to Image Generates a 2D image from a text prompt. This model accepts up to 50 words
Inpainting Changes part of an existing image using a mask plus a text prompt
Outpainting Extends or changes an input image using a prompt and a mask

By default, one run returns 4 images within seconds.

3. Parameters to know

Parameter What it controls Key detail
Prompt What to generate More descriptive gives closer results. E.g. "landscape" → "…with mountains and a river" → "…snow-covered… wooden cabin by the river"
Negative prompt What not to generate E.g. enter "cloud" to keep clouds out
Seed The starting point of the random generation process. A whole number from 0 up Same seed + same prompt = the same image. A different seed gives different images. Use it to make variations while keeping other aspects fixed
Media type Photography vs. illustration Switches the output style
Aspect ratio Image dimensions E.g. 1:1 vs. 16:9
Prompt weight How closely the model follows the prompt Low = more creative. High = follows the prompt exactly. 7.5 is a good balance. At 1, images drifted: a missing cabin, a lake instead of a river, clouds coming back

Why seeds matter: generation involves randomness, so the same prompt can give different images each run. A fixed seed makes the output reproducible.

4. Inpainting walkthrough

  1. Choose Inpainting and select an input image (the demo uses a smartphone with a blank screen).
  2. Add New Mask and paint over the area to change. A brush size slider controls precision (15 was used).
  3. Enter a prompt (the earlier landscape prompt) and Run. Only the masked area, the phone screen, is replaced.

5. Tips for better prompts

  • Be as specific as possible and use descriptive adjectives.
  • Combine several elements to build complex scenes.
  • Use negative prompts to leave out unwanted elements.
  • Try different styles and perspectives.
  • Think about lighting, colors and composition.
  • Reference existing images or artwork for guidance.
  • Break complex scenes into smaller prompts.
  • Iterate and refine based on what you get.

Key Terms

  • Text to image: generating an image from a written prompt.
  • Negative prompt: words describing what the image should not contain.
  • Seed: the number that sets the starting point of the random generation. Fixing it makes results reproducible.
  • Prompt weight: how closely output sticks to the prompt, traded off against creativity.
  • Inpainting / outpainting: editing an existing image within a mask, guided by a prompt.
  • Mask: the painted region that marks which part of the image to change.

Exam Tips

  • Seed = reproducibility. The same seed and prompt give the same image.
  • Prompt weight: low means creative, high means literal. About 7.5 balances the two.
  • Negative prompts exclude content. Inpainting needs a mask.
  • The Getty Images model was trained on NVIDIA Edify and outputs 4K images.

Lesson 3 of 3Beyond Generative AI

What you should be able to do after this unit

  1. Explain the core concepts of agentic AI, physical AI and AI factories, and what sets each apart.
  2. Describe how they work together and complement each other.
  3. Identify real-world applications and the infrastructure needed to deploy them at scale.

1. The Waves of AI

Wave Milestone What it does
Perception AI 2012: AlexNet wins the ImageNet challenge. It was one of the first deep CNNs trained on GPUs, which cut training time sharply Finds patterns and anomalies, and predicts: "Is this a dog or a cat?" "Is there a bad cell in the scan?"
Generative AI 2017: Transformer architecture. 2022: ChatGPT brings generative AI into the mainstream Creates new content from human input
Agentic AI Now Reasons, plans and carries out a series of actions on its own to finish complex tasks
Physical AI Next AI embodied in physical agents that interact with and adapt to the real world

2. Agentic AI

The promise: digital agents that learn from us, perceive, reason through multiple steps, and decide autonomously on our behalf. Example: in a store, one agent works out what customers want, another checks stock, and a third keeps shelves full.

How agents differ from today's AI

  • Specialized: built for a specific task. They're trained and customized, have guardrails, and have domain-specific knowledge.
  • Varying autonomy: different agents have different levels of autonomy.
  • Planning: an agent takes a prompt from a user, a machine or another agent and breaks it into a plan, which is a task list.
  • Tools: web search, vector database lookups, SQL queries, calculations.
  • Guardrails: e.g. a customer-service agent approves claims up to a set amount, and anything above it goes to a human.
  • Memory: the agent remembers what it has done so it can work efficiently.
  • Self-critique and the data flywheel: the agent checks its own work ("is that good enough?") and re-plans until the answer is good. Data from its interactions feeds back to improve the models.

Multi-agent systems

  • The user talks to a higher-level orchestration agent, which directs downstream agents that each have their own tools and data.
  • Example (software development): a bug-tracking agent picks up an issue, a coding agent fixes it, and a technical docs agent documents the change.
  • Orchestrating agents will be an area of significant importance.
  • Agents working together need maximum processing speed, so accelerated computing and AI factories are essential.

Why reasoning matters for agents

Capability Meaning
Contextual understanding Grasps intent, conversation history and background across multiple steps
Multi-source data integration Combines documents, databases and real-time streams
Answer validation Checks that answers are logically coherent and factually sound, which builds trust
Ambiguity resolution Asks clarifying questions or makes probabilistic judgments when information is incomplete or conflicting
Dynamic adaptation Changes strategy as conditions change instead of following rigid scripts

3. Physical AI

  • Industries are moving to software-defined operations, with robotics at every scale. Digital twins and physical AI apply to everything from single assets and factory lines up to fleets, warehouses, networks and city infrastructure.
  • Industries using them include transportation, manufacturing, retail, supply chain and telecommunications.

Three trends driving it

  1. Labor shortage: an estimated 50 million worker gap globally.
  2. Onshoring: new factories are being built closer to home, so speed to deployment is critical. Digital twins let you design and optimize facilities virtually first.
  3. Robotics innovation: a shift from pre-programmed, single-use machines to generalist, autonomous robots that reason and adapt.

Physical AI vs. LLMs

  • LLMs produce text. Physical AI models produce the next best action based on real-time sensor input and intent.
  • It powers three types of robots:
    • infrastructure robots: factories and warehouses
    • general-purpose robots: including humanoids
    • transportation robots: autonomous cars, robo-taxis
  • They're trained in high-fidelity simulations and digital twins, so they learn skills safely at superhuman speed before deployment.

The three-computer solution

Computer Role
1. Supercomputer (training) Trains and fine-tunes AI models on massive datasets
2. Simulation computer Generates synthetic data and tests and validates robot behavior in virtual worlds. This reduces risk and development time
3. Robot runtime computer Embedded in the robot or vehicle for real-time perception, planning and control

Digital twins

  • A dynamic virtual model of a physical system, continuously updated with real-world data. It can represent a robot, a fleet, a factory or a city.
  • Used to monitor performance, predict maintenance, and optimize processes safely before changing the real thing.
  • Used across manufacturing, logistics, energy and transportation. Benefits include proactive maintenance, faster innovation and more resilient operations.

Physical AI + digital twins = a closed loop

  • Digital twins give physical AI agents context and boundaries for safe operation.
  • Physical AI makes digital twins more actionable by learning from real-world data.
  • The loop: the robot acts, its actions are mirrored and analyzed in the twin, and the insights refine both the model and the physical system.

4. AI Factories

The three AI scaling laws

Model performance improves with model size, training data and compute.

Law What drives compute demand
1. Pre-training scaling Bigger models, more data and more compute give better results. This drove the first wave of massive training infrastructure
2. Post-training scaling Extra compute to fine-tune and adapt models for real-world uses
3. Test-time scaling ("long thinking") Reasoning models produce and evaluate many intermediate steps at inference, using up to 100× more compute than traditional inference

Why traditional data centers aren't enough

  1. Complex design: prioritizing use cases, getting high-quality data, and choosing on-prem, cloud or hybrid.
  2. Deployment and operating costs rise as models and data grow.
  3. Time to value: AI needs an optimized end-to-end stack (networking, compute, software). AI puts unique demands on every layer.

Why inference compute is exploding

  1. Bigger models: some have trillions of parameters.
  2. Long thinking / inference-time scaling: reasoning models (e.g. DeepSeek-R1) evaluate several intermediate steps, using up to 100× compute.
  3. Larger context windows: over 100,000 input tokens. Google Gemini can take about 5,000 pages in one prompt, which raises compute and memory bandwidth needs.
  • Demand compounds: compute for state-of-the-art models has grown about 5× per year over the past decade, roughly a billion-fold increase in total.
  • That growth is driven by more powerful GPUs and accelerators and by smarter algorithms.

How an AI factory "manufactures intelligence"

  • Raw materials: foundation models, secure customer data and AI tools.
  • The process: fine-tune and prototype the model for the business's data, vocabulary and workflows.
  • The output: deploy it to production for real-time insights, automation and decision support.
  • The data flywheel: production data flows back for continuous fine-tuning, so every cycle makes the model better.
  • Underneath it all: accelerated computing, high-speed networking and an enterprise data platform.

The full stack an AI factory needs

Layer Role
Compute GPUs and CPUs for training, fine-tuning and inference
Networking High-speed, low-latency InfiniBand and advanced Ethernet connecting compute, storage and users
Software Orchestrates everything: infrastructure management, workload scheduling, frameworks, dev tools, monitoring
Ecosystem partners Integrated and validated together to cut complexity and speed up time to value

Key Terms

  • Perception AI: AI that recognizes patterns and makes predictions. It began with AlexNet (2012).
  • Agentic AI: autonomous agents that reason, plan, use tools and act, with memory and self-critique.
  • Orchestration agent: the top-level agent that coordinates the downstream specialist agents.
  • Data flywheel: interaction or production data fed back to keep improving the model.
  • Physical AI: models that output the next best action from sensor input, for robots and vehicles.
  • Digital twin: a live virtual copy of a physical system, kept updated with real data.
  • Three-computer solution: training supercomputer + simulation computer + robot runtime computer.
  • Scaling laws: pre-training, post-training and test-time ("long thinking").
  • AI factory: purpose-built, full-stack infrastructure that turns data and models into intelligence at scale.

Exam Tips

  • Know the order: Perception (AlexNet 2012) → Generative (Transformer 2017, ChatGPT 2022) → Agentic → Physical.
  • What agents have: planning (task lists), tools, guardrails, memory and self-critique.
  • Physical AI outputs actions, not text. Know the three robot types and the three computers (train, simulate, run).
  • Test-time scaling / long thinking uses up to 100× more inference compute.
  • Three scaling laws: pre-training, post-training, test-time.
  • Inference demand is driven by bigger models, reasoning (long thinking) and larger context windows.
  • AI factory stack: compute + networking (InfiniBand / Ethernet) + software, all validated together.
